Nord Garage · colour study · rev A

Three ways to paint the same garage.

Same layout, same photography, same words — only the palette changes. Judge them on the interface below rather than on the swatches: a colour that looks great as a square can fall apart the moment it has to carry a phone number, a price and a dirty engine bay.

How these were built

  • One accent, strictly rationed. Roughly a tenth of the surface. The reference mockup used bright yellow on every element, which is exactly why it read as noisy.
  • Contrast measured, not guessed. Every ratio below is calculated; body text clears WCAG AA (4.5:1) in all three.
  • Photography first. A garage site lives on photos of metal, foam and paint. The neutrals are chosen to sit behind them, not to fight them.

Night & Brass

Deep navy with muted brass. The one already built — kept here as the control so the other two have something to beat.

Why it works

  • Night is a workshop at 17:00 in November. Selfoss is dark half the year; the site matches the light your customers actually stand in when they call you.
  • Brass is warmth without alarm. It carries the same "premium service" signal as gold in a hotel lobby, but desaturated enough not to shout like the yellow in the original mockup.
  • Photos glow on it. Dark navy pushes chrome, headlights and wet paint forward — the tiles do the work, the background disappears.

Watch out

  • Everyone's premium garage is dark. It is a safe, well-trodden choice — it will not be mistaken for anyone, but it will not stand out on the street either.
  • Brass needs discipline. The moment it lands on every heading it turns back into the mockup you rejected.

Why it works

  • The colour is already in your building. Torque wrenches, jack stands, hazard cones, hi-vis — orange is the native colour of a working garage. It reads as honest equipment, not as decoration.
  • Grey is neutral in a way navy is not. Navy is a mood; graphite is a material. Photos of any car colour — white, red, silver — sit on it without clashing, which matters when the board fills with real customer cars.
  • Orange is the strongest "press this" colour there is. Highest visibility per square centimetre of any warm hue, so the phone button can be small and still be the first thing the eye lands on.
  • It separates you from the dealership. Every franchised service centre in the country is blue or silver. Orange on graphite reads independent, hands-on, cheaper without looking cheap.

Watch out

  • Orange has a volume knob and no brake. Over roughly a tenth of the screen it stops meaning "important" and starts meaning "discount tyres". The budget bar below is not a suggestion.
  • It fights red cars. If your best photo is a red car, the tile caption needs to sit on a scrim, not on the paint.
  • Not a luxury signal. If you ever want to court the Range Rover crowd, A ages better.

Why it works

  • Nobody else in the trade is doing it. Auto service sites are dark by reflex. A light one reads as a clinic — clean floors, itemised invoices, no surprises — which is precisely what people fear they will not get from a garage.
  • It is the honest local colour. Warm paper, cold petrol green-blue: Icelandic daylight and glacier water. It belongs in Selfoss in a way a black showroom does not.
  • Phones are used outdoors. Most of your visitors are on a phone, often in daylight or in a car. A light interface stays readable where a dark one becomes a mirror.
  • Petrol is trust without corporate blue. Dark teal carries the same competence signal as navy but avoids the insurance-company look, and it holds 7.6:1 against paper — strong enough for body text, not just buttons.

Watch out

  • It demands better photography. Dark backgrounds hide a mediocre snapshot; white does not. Every tile needs decent light and a clean crop, or the whole page looks unfinished.
  • Rust is decoration only. #B4693A lands at 3.7:1 on paper — fine for icons, numbers and large headings, never for paragraphs.
  • Least "automotive" at first glance. It signals care and order rather than speed and power. Right for service and rental; the weakest of the three if you ever pivot to tuning.

On the building

Petrol on off-white is the cheapest sign to keep looking new — pale surfaces do not show Icelandic dust and salt film the way gloss black does. Reverse it after dark: white letters on petrol for the illuminated panel.

Which one to take

DirectionTake it ifLeave it ifReads as
A · Night & Brass You want the safe, premium default and plan to compete on quality of finish. You want to look different from every other garage site. Established. Careful, a little formal.
B · Graphite & Signal You want people to call, today, and you like being read as a real workshop. Your photos are mostly red cars, or you are chasing a luxury clientele. Capable. Direct, energetic, independent.
C · Nordic Daylight You can get good photographs and want to be the tidy, transparent option in town. Your pictures are grab shots on a phone in a dim bay. Trustworthy. Clean, local, unhurried.

My recommendation

B — Graphite & Signal. It is the only one of the three that is both distinctive and unfussy about your photography, and for a business whose whole funnel ends in someone tapping a phone number, the strongest possible call button is worth more than atmosphere. Keep C in your pocket: if the photo shoot comes back bright and clean, it becomes the better site.
